What it means for retail

  • Competition and unit economics are the retail killers. In marketplaces, competition caused 51.3% and unit economics 25.1% of 335 failures, and the two feed each other often intertwines with competition as startups burn capital on customer acquisition and supplier subsidies to fend off rivals Loot Drop. In the base's own classification of 278 retail rows, competition and unit economics again lead (Loot Drop, an inference).
  • Consumer is the most exposed sector to unit economics. Consumer companies were especially vulnerable (101 failures) Loot Drop. In the danger matrix the Consumer row is heaviest on Crushed and Math Problem.
  • Marketplaces die after traction, not at launch. The 5.4-year average lifespan means most marketplaces fail after achieving initial traction, not at launch. Loot Drop So a premortem for a live marketplace matters more than one for a new idea.
  • Demand is rarely the problem. Only 8.4 percent failed from no market need Loot Drop. Proving shoppers want it is not proving you can serve them profitably.
  • Regulation kills slowly. The Outlaw has the longest median lifespan, seven years. Payments, lending, quick commerce labour models and cross-border trade are the retail areas most exposed.

The self-test

The Premortem answers each question in one line before writing the failure story.

  1. Hallucination. When shoppers or sellers hear about it, do they ask how to start, or only say it sounds interesting?
  2. Bonfire. If no new money came, how long until current revenue covers costs?
  3. Civil War. When the founders, client sponsors or investors disagree, is there a clear decider?
  4. Crushed. Why did the last three lost customers, sellers or brands choose a competitor, and do we know?
  5. Lemon. Has the delivery, catalogue, search or payments capability been proven at production volume, or only in a pilot?
  6. Outlaw. If every rule were enforced tomorrow (labour, payments, consumer protection, customs), would the model survive?
  7. Math Problem. After realistic cost gains, what would shoppers or sellers have to pay for a 20% gross margin, and would they?

Premortem checklist

Answered in one line each, with evidence or unknown, before the story is told.

  1. Which antipattern is most likely, using the seven self-test questions in antipatterns.md?
  2. Which two or three failure modes above are closest to this idea, and which case from the same region is the nearest match (retail-failures-by-region.md)?
  3. Is demand measured without incentives, or only with them (F4)?
  4. Does the case depend on a rule, a platform, a public rail or a single partner that someone else controls (F5, F6, F16)?
  5. Does it require density, automation or headcount before the orders exist (F1, F2, F13)?
  6. Which side of the product, partner or channel loses if this works (F7)?
  7. Who could copy it faster because they already own the customer or supply (F12)?
  8. What is the burn against revenue, and the dated path to close it (F3)?
  9. Are the reported numbers the ones that reach the bank (F15)?
  10. Which region's case is this borrowed from, and does the transfer check hold here (F14)?

Marketplace red flags

From Loot Drop's marketplace deep dive, paraphrased.

  • Red flags. Subsidies with no end date. An advantage that is only first-mover status or more funding. New categories or cities before the core is profitable. Acquisition cost rising with scale. No reason for either side to stay if a rival offers better terms.
  • Metrics. Take rate after all discounts. Cohort lifetime value against fully loaded acquisition cost. Share of listings that transact. Repeat rate. Contribution margin per transaction.
